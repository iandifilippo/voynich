# c06 — "tolor means fertilization" (r/voynich)

## What was claimed

u/Worldmaster777 noticed that the string *tolor* occurs three times: *tolor* as the first word
of f38r and *otolor* as a label on f67r2 and on f77v. He reads all three pictures as being
about fertilization (f38r as genitals rather than a plant, f67r2 as months of pregnancy,
f77v as a uterus and fallopian tubes) and concludes *tolor* = fertilization. From that he
builds a grammar: *o-* works like "the", the gallows mark part of speech (*t-* noun, *k-*
verb), and final *-l* / *-r* mark feminine / masculine.

## What I think

I can't test the reading of the pictures with the text, and the anachronism problem has
already been raised in the thread (egg cells and fallopian tubes were described centuries
later). What I *can* test is everything that leans on the text: whether three occurrences
in that layout are unusual, and whether the grammar he derives leaves the traces a real
grammar would leave.

## What I did

- Checked the occurrences in ZL.
- Look-elsewhere: counted every word that opens a paragraph and has an *o-* twin used as a
  label, with 2-5 occurrences in total, i.e. the exact layout of *tolor* / *otolor*.
- Gender: real gender endings make neighbours agree (Italian *la casa bianca*, Latin *-am
  -am*). I measured whether adjacent words share *-l* or *-r* more than they would if the
  words in each line were shuffled, and ran the same test on Italian and Latin as a check
  that it can see agreement when it's there.
- *o-* as article, *t-/k-* as noun/verb: compared the neighbours of minimal pairs (*daiin* /
  *odaiin*, *otedy* / *okedy*, *chol* / *chor*) with random words of similar frequency, and
  with two control prefixes (*q-*, *y-*) nobody has given a meaning to.

## Result

- **The occurrences are right**: *tolor* opens the first paragraph of f38r; *otolor* is a
  label on f67r2 (one of 64 labels on that page) and on f77v (one of 10).
- **The layout is common.** 1652 word types occur 2-5 times. Six other words have exactly the
  same layout: *torain/otorain, tokol/otokol, fcheody/ofcheody, pairar/opairar,
  palar/opalar, porain/oporain*. It follows from two well-known habits: paragraphs open with
  a gallows word that's usually unique (61% of opening words occur nowhere else), and labels
  favour *ot-/ok-/op-*. *tolor* is one of seven, not one of one.
- **No gender agreement.** Italian *-a/-o*: z = 16. Latin *-a/-us*: z = 3.1, *-am/-um*: z = 4.3.
  VMS *-l/-r*: z = 0.8 overall, −1.1 in Currier B, 2.1 in Currier A (p = 0.017, borderline
  after correcting for three tests, and an order of magnitude weaker than Italian).
- **Gallows don't behave like part-of-speech markers.** Words that differ only in *t/k* have
  the *most* similar contexts of any pairs tested (2.0× random). A noun and its verb would
  have different neighbours. *o-* pairs (1.5×) look exactly like the control prefixes *q-*
  (1.5×) and *y-* (1.4×): nothing marks *o-* out as an article.

## So what

The three-occurrence coincidence is an artefact of where gallows words and labels sit, and
the grammar built on it doesn't show up in the text. The one faint signal (some *-l/-r*
agreement in Currier A) is worth keeping an eye on as a dialect feature, not as gender.
The general method, looking at words with few occurrences and asking where they sit, is a
good one; it just needs the look-elsewhere count done first.
