# c10 — "An astronomical-astrological code" (voynich.ninja)

## What was claimed

Antonio García Jiménez: there is no language here at all. Each glyph is a sign in a star
catalogue for herbal medicine. *o* is the degree of the sphere where the star sits, *q*
before it marks the star rising, *e, ee, eee, eeee* (his c, cc, ccc, cccc) are days of the
Moon through the 28 lunar mansions, the gallows are coordinates and "always at first of the
paragraphs", repeated words are stars that are almost in the same place, and the text was
generated with a volvelle.

## What I think

It can't be tested as a decipherment because it doesn't decode anything. But it says where
signs should be, and that can be counted. It's also closer in spirit to where the evidence
has been pointing (a procedure, not a language) than most readings.

## Result

| claim | count |
|---|---|
| gallows always start paragraphs | 83% of paragraphs open with a gallows, but those are 3.4% of all gallows. *k* and *t* (16 000 of them) are spread through the text; only *p* and *f* belong to first lines (81% and 78%) |
| *q* is a prefix of *o* | 97.8% of *q* are followed by *o*. True, and long known |
| *e*-runs count days of the Moon | *e* 9134, *ee* 3945, *eee* 350, *eeee* 6, never more. A count of days up to 28 would need a way to write more than four |
| repeats are neighbouring stars | repeats are no commoner in the stars section (8.1 per 1000) than in the herbal (9.5) or balneological (11.8) |

## So what

One observation is right (*q* before *o*), one is half right (two of the four gallows do
mark paragraph openings, which is real structure), two don't fit. As a whole it doesn't
explain the counts, but "the glyphs are slots filled by a device" is a hypothesis worth
keeping: a volvelle or a table is one way to get rigid word structure, and it predicts
that positions within a word are filled independently. That is testable and it's on the list.
